Research

Can a weather model's reasoning survive a second opinion?

Deep learning forecasters like GraphCast and AIFS now rival or beat classical numerical weather prediction on many metrics — but their internal reasoning is opaque. My PhD builds attribution methods that trace a forecast back to the input fields that produced it, so operational meteorologists can tell physically grounded reasoning from spurious correlation.

Method

From a raw gradient to a trustworthy explanation

Attribution methods for physical fields face a specific problem: raw input gradients are noisy, and models predicting continuous dynamic fields (not single class scores) don't fit classical saliency recipes cleanly. The pipeline below is how I get from "here's a forecast" to "here's what drove it."

01

Input field

Geopotential, humidity, wind, temperature — pressure-level fields fed to the forecaster (ERA5-derived, via xarray).

02

Forward model

GraphCast, UNETR++, AIFS or AROME predicts the next physical state.

03

Noisy gradients

Backpropagate to the input. SmoothGrad / VarGrad average many noised passes to reduce variance.

04

WassersteinGrad

Wasserstein barycenters aggregate attribution distributions across samples, denoising explanations for dynamic fields.

05

Explanation map

A spatially coherent attribution field, legible to a forecaster reading it like a weather chart.

01

Explaining Neural Weather Models

Gradient-based attributions for deep forecasters — PhD research

A framework that interrogates deep forecasting models by tracing the influence of input fields (geopotential, humidity, wind) onto predicted variables via SmoothGrad, VarGrad and WassersteinGrad. The goal: separate real physical reasoning from spurious correlation, and make the results legible to operational meteorologists.
