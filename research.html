Research

Reading neural weather forecasts backwards.

Data-driven models now match or beat numerical weather prediction on many scores, at a fraction of the cost. But when one of them predicts heavy rain over Paris, a forecaster has no way to ask what in the current state of the atmosphere made it say so.

My PhD at the Institut de Mathématiques de Toulouse builds attribution methods for these models: tools that trace a forecast back to the input fields that produced it, so that physically grounded reasoning can be told apart from spurious correlation, and read like a weather chart.

Three tests for an explanation

  • Faithful

    It should reflect what the model actually computes: hiding the regions it points to should change the forecast more than hiding random ones.

  • Stable

    A tiny perturbation of the input barely changes the forecast. It should not send the explanation across the map.

  • Physical

    It should agree with how the atmosphere works: influence coming from upstream, at plausible distances, differently at each level.

Why weather is a hard case

Gradient attribution methods were designed for photographs: a static image on a pixel grid. The atmosphere is a continuous, advective, physically constrained field, and modern models forecast it autoregressively, feeding each prediction back in as the next input. Gradients travel back through every one of those steps, and whatever distortion each step adds accumulates.

The data are kilometre-scale too. The WassersteinGrad experiments use TITAN, derived from Météo-France’s operational AROME model: a 512 × 640 grid over France at about 2.5 km, with 21 surface and upper-air channels.
